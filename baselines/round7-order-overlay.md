# Pricing page for a veterinary practice-management tool

A pricing page rewrite for practice-management software, reordered around the three questions managers brought to demo calls.

- Client: A veterinary software company
- Role: Product designer
- Year: 2025
- Duration: 5 weeks
- Status: Shipped

## Overview

The product is practice-management software for small animal clinics. Practice managers read its pricing page while they compare it against two competitors and before they book a demo. I was the product designer on a five-week rewrite of that page for the company that makes it, working with Marco Ilves, sales lead. The scope was the one page, built on a marketing-site template the team would not change.

## Problem

The client reported that the pricing page was not converting and that they thought it was too long. The report was right about the symptom and wrong about the cause.

Length was not what stood in the way. Managers arrived with three questions: what it costs per vet, whether it replaces the records system they already run, and who does the data migration. The page made each one hard to answer, and two blocks of feature copy sat between the price and the first clinic that had migrated.

Managers did not know whether it replaced the records system they ran, because nothing on the site said what it replaced. Who does the data migration was answered in a footnote under the FAQ.

I reached this by talking to the managers who use the page. The cost landed on sales: they answered the same three questions on every demo call, about eleven calls a week.

## Approach

The marketing site ran on a template the team would not change, so every section of the page had to be one of its existing blocks. The work was therefore in what each block said and where it sat.

The three changes differ in kind. Two moved an answer to where a manager already looks: the plan prices went above the feature grid, and migration came out of the FAQ into its own section. The third added a statement the site had never made, naming the records system the product replaces, and it departs from the rest of the site, where no page names a competitor. The migration change depends on the price move, so it is read after it.

## Solution

### Plan prices and the feature grid

The client's request, in their words:

> "The pricing page is not converting and we think it is too long."

Two blocks of feature copy sat between the price and the clinics, and the plan prices sat below two screens of it. Every manager we sat with scrolled straight past the features looking for a number, and two of them left before they found it. I put the plan prices above the feature grid, so a manager sees what it costs without scrolling past anything.

I considered a price link in the nav and dropped it. It answers the question by sending someone somewhere else.

### Records system reference

Every other page on the site avoids naming a competitor. On this page I name the records system the product replaces, and I did it on purpose.

Managers did not know whether it replaced the records system they ran. They were already running one and assumed this sat on top of it, so the question was always whether they would be paying for both, and nothing on the site said what it replaced. Keeping to the convention would have left that question open. A manager now reads which system it replaces and stops asking whether they would be running two. I weighed a comparison table and dropped it: it invites a feature argument, and the question was not about features.

### Data migration section

Nobody has to open an accordion to find out who moves their records.

Who does the data migration was answered in a footnote under the FAQ, the last thing on the page and the third question on every call. A clinic that has typed in six years of records does not treat it as a detail, so I gave the answer its own section. This change depends on the price move, so it is read after it. I considered promoting the FAQ entry to the top of the accordion, but it is still a question someone has to go looking for.

## Outcome

The client set out to fix a pricing page that was not converting and that they thought was too long. What changed is in how the team works: the three questions stopped coming up on calls, and sales sends the page now instead of talking through it.

The client said afterwards:

> "We send the pricing page now instead of walking people through it."
